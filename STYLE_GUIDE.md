# Northward Meridian Editorial Style Guide

## Purpose

Northward Meridian helps people make complicated everyday decisions using evidence, math, practical frameworks, and decision tools.

Every guide should leave the reader more capable of making a decision than they were before arriving.

Northward Meridian is not a generic informational blog.

The central editorial question is:

> What decision is the reader trying to make, and what information would materially improve that decision?

---

## Core Standard

Every guide must address one clear decision or decision-oriented question.

Common patterns include:

- Is X worth it?
- Should I do X or Y?
- Does X actually save money?
- Should I repair or replace X?
- Should I pay for X?
- When does X make financial sense?
- Does X actually improve Y?

Examples:

- Is Your Premium Credit Card Worth the Annual Fee?
- Should I Use 0% Financing or Pay Cash?
- Is It Cheaper to Hand-Wash Dishes or Use a Dishwasher?
- Does a Tonneau Cover Save Enough Gas to Pay for Itself?
- Should You Repair or Replace Your Water Heater?

Avoid articles that merely define a subject without helping the reader make a decision.

---

## The Northward Meridian Approach

A strong guide should generally help the reader:

1. Understand the decision.
2. Identify the variables that matter.
3. Evaluate the available evidence.
4. Understand the tradeoffs.
5. See how different real-world circumstances change the answer.
6. Reach a defensible conclusion for their own situation.
7. Know what to do next.

The guide should not manufacture certainty when the correct answer depends on the reader's circumstances.

---

## Guide Structure

Recent guides generally follow this structure:

1. Category and title
2. Description
3. Updated date and reading time
4. Guided entry or decision context
5. Calculator, reality check, or decision tool when useful
6. Short answer / why the decision matters
7. Research-backed sections
8. Real-world scenarios
9. Decision checklist
10. Questions to ask
11. Key takeaways
12. Sources
13. Related decisions

This is a framework, not a mandatory article template.

Do not add a section merely because another guide contains it.

The structure should serve the decision.

---

## Guided Entry

When appropriate, begin by helping readers identify which version of the decision they are facing.

A guided entry may distinguish circumstances such as:

- buying vs. already owning
- repairing vs. replacing
- short-term vs. long-term ownership
- high vs. low usage
- financing vs. paying cash
- convenience vs. pure economics

The purpose is not to create an unnecessary quiz.

It should quickly orient the reader toward the parts of the guide most relevant to them.

---

## Bottom Line / Short Answer

Give the reader a useful answer early.

The short answer should:

- directly address the decision
- state the conditions that materially change the answer
- avoid false precision
- avoid burying the conclusion beneath qualifications
- make clear when there is no universal answer

Preferred:

> A true 0% loan can beat paying cash when the financed price is the same, fees are negligible, and you keep the cash earning a return. A cash discount or financing fees can quickly reverse the result.

Avoid:

> There are many factors to consider, and ultimately the answer depends on your individual circumstances.

The second statement may be true, but it does not help the reader.

## Editorial Principles

### Clarity Over Complexity

Use straightforward language.

Explain technical terminology when it is necessary to understand the decision.

Do not use jargon merely to sound authoritative.

### Usefulness Over Volume

Do not add content merely to increase article length.

Every section should help the reader:

- understand
- compare
- calculate
- evaluate
- decide
- act

### Evidence Over Intuition

Use evidence for factual claims.

When evidence is weak, mixed, or highly situation-dependent, say so.

### Accuracy Over Speed

Verify factual claims before publication.

Never invent:

- figures
- statistics
- quotations
- citations
- regulations
- specifications
- firsthand experience

### Conditions Over Universal Recommendations

Many Northward Meridian decisions do not have one correct answer.

Explain which conditions favor each option.

The reader should understand what variables would cause the recommendation to change.

### Real Value Over Advertised Value

When evaluating products, services, credits, rewards, efficiency improvements, or other benefits, distinguish advertised or theoretical value from value a person is realistically likely to receive.

### Balanced Tradeoffs

Present legitimate benefits and disadvantages.

Do not disguise marketing language as analysis.

### Transparency About Uncertainty

State when the result depends on:

- assumptions
- uncertain estimates
- incomplete research
- individual behavior
- changing regulations
- future prices
- investment returns
- product lifespan
- local conditions

### Human Accountability

AI may assist with research, calculations, outlining, drafting, coding, and editing.

A human should review each guide before publication for:

- accuracy
- usefulness
- misleading claims
- calculator behavior
- tone
- source quality
- formatting

## Voice

Northward Meridian should sound:

- calm
- practical
- direct
- credible
- thoughtful
- analytical
- nonjudgmental

Northward Meridian should not sound:

- sensational
- promotional
- overly casual
- academic for its own sake
- artificially authoritative
- certain when the evidence is uncertain
- like generic AI-generated content

---

## Writing Rules

### Titles

Use natural decision-focused titles.

Preferred:

- Is Your Premium Credit Card Worth the Annual Fee?
- Should I Use 0% Financing or Pay Cash?
- Does a Roof Rack Use Enough Extra Gas That You Should Remove It?
- Is It More Fuel Efficient to Idle or Turn Your Car Off?

Avoid:

- The Ultimate Guide to Premium Credit Cards
- Everything You Need to Know About Roof Racks
- 17 Shocking Ways to Save Gas
- Best Financial Decisions You Need to Make Today

The title should reflect the actual question the guide answers.

---

## Paragraphs

Prefer short, readable paragraphs.

Two to four sentences is often appropriate.

Use longer paragraphs when a complete argument requires them.

Do not artificially split every sentence into its own paragraph.

---

## Headings

Headings should reflect the reader's decision or question.

Preferred:

- What 0% Financing Really Costs
- When Paying Cash Wins
- How Much Fuel Does a Roof Rack Use?
- What Changes the Break-Even Point?
- When Repairing Makes More Sense

Avoid vague headings such as:

- Introduction
- More Information
- Other Things
- Additional Details
- Conclusion

---

## Lists

Use lists when they improve scanning or comparison.

Do not convert every section into bullets.

---

## Tables

Use tables when they make comparisons easier to understand.

Good uses include:

- costs
- benefits
- assumptions
- options
- eligibility
- timelines
- scenarios
- tradeoffs

Tables should simplify the decision rather than decorate the page.

---

## Scenarios

Real-world scenarios are encouraged when the answer changes substantially based on circumstances.

A useful scenario should demonstrate how the decision framework behaves.

Examples might include:

- low vs. high annual mileage
- small vs. large cash discounts
- short vs. long ownership periods
- low vs. high utility rates
- occasional vs. heavy usage

Do not invent scenarios that imply they represent actual people or firsthand experience.

---

## Calculators and Decision Tools

Calculators are a major Northward Meridian differentiation strategy.

Do not include a calculator merely because one can be built.

A calculator should expose variables that materially affect the decision.

Useful tools may include:

- calculators
- break-even tools
- decision trees
- scorecards
- comparison tools
- reality checks
- checklists

A numeric calculator generally should contain:

1. Inputs
2. Result metrics
3. Interpretation
4. Break-even or threshold information when useful
5. Important caveats

The calculator's math should align with the reasoning in the guide.

Do not use simplified calculator logic that contradicts the article's underlying model.

Do not imply greater precision than the available evidence supports.

---

## Calculator Testing

Before publishing a calculator:

- test obvious equality or baseline cases
- test cases where Option A should clearly win
- test cases where Option B should clearly win
- test extreme but valid inputs
- test zero values when permitted
- test break-even calculations
- verify displayed interpretations against the actual math
- verify assumptions are clearly communicated

When a break-even result is based on the calculator model, use the same underlying model rather than an inconsistent shortcut whenever practical.

---

## Evidence and Sources

Use authoritative primary sources whenever possible.

Examples:

- government agencies
- regulators
- manufacturers
- official product documentation
- research organizations
- academic research
- technical standards
- original datasets

Secondary sources may be useful for:

- context
- interpretation
- consumer experiences
- identifying competing viewpoints

Do not use a weaker secondary source for a core factual claim when an authoritative primary source is readily available.

Every factual claim that may change over time should be checked before publication.

Never fabricate citations or URLs.

---

## Financial Content

Financial guides are educational and should not be presented as individualized financial advice.

Clearly distinguish:

- facts
- estimates
- assumptions
- modeled results
- uncertain future returns

Use authoritative financial and regulatory sources when possible, such as:

- CFPB
- FTC
- Federal Reserve
- IRS
- SEC
- official issuer disclosures

Avoid guarantees about:

- investment returns
- approval
- savings
- credit scores
- future interest rates
- financial outcomes

---

## Firsthand Experience

Do not imply Northward Meridian personally tested, purchased, visited, owned, or used something unless that occurred.

Use honest phrasing such as:

- Based on the published specifications...
- According to the manufacturer's documentation...
- According to the regulator...
- Using the assumptions below...
- Under this scenario...

Do not write:

- We tested every...
- In our experience...
- We personally found...

unless those statements are true.

---

## Original Value Requirement

Every guide should provide meaningful value beyond summarizing existing search results.

Possible differentiators include:

- calculator
- decision framework
- break-even analysis
- worked example
- comparison table
- checklist
- decision tree
- original analysis
- original dataset
- useful synthesis of primary research

A guide should answer:

> Why should this page exist when search results on this subject already exist?

If there is no strong answer, reconsider the topic or improve the concept before publishing.

---

## Related Decisions

Related guides should represent genuinely adjacent decisions.

Do not add unrelated links merely for SEO.

Good internal links help a reader continue solving the larger problem they are already working through.

When appropriate, related relationships should be reciprocal.

---

## Search Intent

Write for the decision the reader is trying to make.

Do not distort the guide to repeat keywords.

A guide should satisfy the search intent more completely, clearly, or usefully than existing results.

The strongest opportunity may be a relatively narrow query rather than a broad high-volume keyword.

---

## Review Checklist

Before publishing, confirm:

- [ ] The guide addresses one clear decision.
- [ ] The reader receives a useful short answer early.
- [ ] The important variables that change the answer are identified.
- [ ] Legitimate tradeoffs are presented fairly.
- [ ] Factual claims have been verified.
- [ ] Time-sensitive information is current.
- [ ] Primary sources were used where practical.
- [ ] No citations, numbers, URLs, or quotations were invented.
- [ ] The article does not claim firsthand experience that did not occur.
- [ ] The guide provides original value beyond a generic summary.
- [ ] Any calculator or decision tool has been tested.
- [ ] Calculator results agree with the article's reasoning.
- [ ] Assumptions and uncertainty are clear.
- [ ] The reader knows what to do next.
- [ ] Related guides are genuinely relevant.
- [ ] The guide has been reviewed by a human.
- [ ] The rendered page has been visually checked.

---

## Brand Terminology

Use:

- Northward Meridian — company or publisher
- Guides — published decision-focused resources
- Guided Entry — interactive or context-setting entry point when used
- Bottom Line — concise answer stored in guide metadata
- Meridian Framework — the broader decision-making approach
- Related Decisions — related guide recommendations

Avoid referring to Northward Meridian guides as generic blog posts.

---

## Final Editorial Test

Before publishing, ask:

> Did we help the reader make the decision, or did we merely write about the topic?

If the page primarily explains the topic without materially improving the decision, it is not finished.
